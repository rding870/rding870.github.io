Distilling a vision-language-action model for real-time robot control

Big VLA models are accurate but too slow to drive a robot in real time. What if we distill one to make it faster?

The model: LingBot-VA

LingBot-VA proposes video world modeling as a foundation for robot learning. The intuition is that by predicting how the world will look in the future, the model can infer what actions the robot should take to reach those future states. Instead of predicting actions directly from the current camera observation, LingBot-VA first predicts future visual states and then uses an inverse dynamics model to generate the corresponding robot actions.

Architecturally, LingBot-VA uses two transformer backbones: a large video expert and a smaller action expert. Both are diffusion models trained with flow matching, and they are connected through a Mixture-of-Transformers architecture that lets them share information. The overall model is autoregressive across chunks of video and actions rather than individual frames.

Building the evaluation pipeline

Before distilling anything, I needed a fixed, reproducible benchmark so that every model variant is scored on exactly the same scenes.

  1. Choose the tasks. 10 of RoboTwin's 50 tasks, drawn randomly.
  2. Freeze the scenes. For each task, sweep scene seeds upward from 100000 and run RoboTwin's scripted expert (a motion planner). Keep the first 20 seeds the expert accepts. This guarantees every scene is actually completable.
  3. Build the eval driver. It wraps LeRobot's rollout loop and, per episode, records success, step count, errors, per-chunk inference latency, the env-vs-policy time split, SHA-256 fingerprints of every action chunk and observation batch, and a video.
  4. Run the teacher baseline on all 200 scenes.

Method: distilling LingBot-VA with DMD2

Goal

During inference, LingBot-VA starts from random noise and gradually denoises it into video and action predictions. The number of denoising steps, which I will call NFE, sets the inference cost. The goal is to distill the NFE-30 teacher into students that use only 16 or 8 steps while maintaining similar task success.

Importantly, the student isn't a smaller network. It starts from the same weights but learns new LoRA adapters that let it produce good predictions in fewer steps. To train them, I use a simplified version of Distribution Matching Distillation 2 (DMD2), which trains the student's output distribution to resemble the teacher's rather than forcing it to reproduce individual teacher samples.

The three model roles

Teacher (real score model)
The original RoboTwin-finetuned LingBot-VA. Frozen during training. It represents the distribution we want the student to learn.
Student (generator)
The model we're improving. It generates future video and actions in fewer denoising steps and receives gradients from the distribution-matching loss. Trained via the gen LoRA adapter.
Fake score model
A second trainable LoRA adapter whose job is to estimate the student's current output distribution. Trained via the fake LoRA adapter.

All three roles share the same underlying transformer weights, with different adapters enabled depending on which role is active. The original DMD2 paper also includes a GAN discriminator; my implementation omits it (more on that below).

Step 1 Generate a partially denoised sample

Suppose I'm training an NFE-8 student, so at inference it takes eight denoising steps. At each step the student predicts a velocity that says how to change the current noisy sample to make it less noisy.

During training, instead of always running all eight steps, I randomly choose a denoising position. If I choose step 4, I run the student through the first three steps without tracking gradients, leaving a partially denoised video and action representation.

Step 2 Predict the clean output from the intermediate sample

I pass the partially denoised sample through the student once more to predict another velocity. Instead of using it to take one small denoising step, I use it to estimate what the completely clean output would look like.

In flow matching, a noisy sample at noise level σ is

xσ = (1 − σ) x0 + σ ε,

where x0 is the clean sample and ε is random noise. Since the model predicts the velocity v = ε − x0, I can estimate the clean output in one shot:

x̂0 = xσ − σ v(xσ, σ).

This estimate is not necessarily identical to what the student would produce after all eight steps. But because the velocity prediction is differentiable, I can compute a loss on the clean estimate and backpropagate through this one model evaluation. Backpropagating through the entire eight-step rollout would require storing activations from every step, which is far more memory. By randomly choosing different denoising positions across iterations, the student receives feedback throughout its sampling trajectory while only paying for one step of backprop at a time.

Step 3 Compute the distribution-matching loss

Now I need to measure how closely the student's clean estimate matches the teacher's output distribution. Rather than comparing against one exact teacher sample, DMD2 compares the denoising predictions of the teacher and the fake score model:

  1. Add fresh random noise to the student's estimated clean output at a randomly chosen noise level.
  2. Pass this noisy sample through both the teacher and the fake score model.
  3. The teacher predicts how the sample should be denoised according to its learned distribution. The fake score model predicts how it should be denoised according to the student's current distribution.
  4. The difference between the two predictions is a gradient that pushes the student toward the teacher's distribution.

This is why the fake score model is necessary: the teacher tells us what its own distribution looks like, but only the fake score model carries information about what the student currently generates.

My implementation computes separate distribution-matching terms for video and actions and combines them as

LG = 0.5 LDM, video + 0.5 LDM, action.

The teacher's video predictions use classifier-free guidance with scale 5.0, so the student is learning to approximate the guided teacher. I then backpropagate LG through the student's clean estimate from Step 2 and update only the student's gen adapter. Neither the teacher nor the fake score model is touched in this step.

Step 4 Train the fake score model five times

After the student update, its output distribution has shifted slightly, so the fake score model may no longer accurately represent it. Since the distribution-matching loss depends on an accurate estimate of the student's distribution, the fake score model has to catch up.

I take the student's predicted clean video and actions, detach them so no gradient flows back into the student, and add random noise. Because I know both the generated sample and the noise I added, the correct denoising velocity is simply vtarget = ε − x0. The fake score model is trained to match it with an ordinary flow-matching loss,

LF = LFM, video + LFM, action,

updating only the fake adapter. I run five fake-score updates for every student update, the two-time-scale update rule (TTUR). Each of the five uses the freshly updated weights from the previous one, and fresh noise and noise levels can be sampled each time; the detached student samples are reused. The student stays fixed throughout.

Five is a hyperparameter, not a mathematical requirement. The point is to give the fake score model enough opportunity to track the student's moving distribution before the next generator update. The fake-score loss is an auxiliary objective: a lower value means the model denoises student samples better, not that the student's robot actions are improving.

Step 5 Repeat across denoising positions

After one generator update and five fake-score updates, I repeat with a new random denoising position. The next iteration might pick step 6 instead of step 4: run the first five student steps without gradients, use the sixth velocity prediction to estimate the clean output, compute the distribution-matching gradient with the teacher and fake score model, update the student once, then train the fake score model five more times.

Over many iterations the student receives feedback at different noise levels and positions throughout its schedule. The hope is that these local improvements add up to a better complete trajectory when the student runs all eight steps at inference. Because training uses intermediate clean estimates rather than differentiating through the full rollout, a better training objective doesn't automatically guarantee better task success.

Step 6 Export and evaluate the student

When training finishes, I discard the fake score model and keep the trained student LoRA. At inference the student runs its full reduced-NFE schedule (all eight steps for an NFE-8 student) to generate video and action chunks in the same closed loop as the teacher. I evaluate on the same 200 frozen RoboTwin scenes, measuring success rate and latency.

Crucially, each trained student is compared not only against the NFE-30 teacher but also against an untrained control at the same NFE. Simply reducing the step count already makes inference faster, even with no distillation. The student has to beat its same-NFE control to show that the distillation procedure itself adds value.

Two limitations of the current setup

Why the implementation omits the GAN loss

DMD2 adds a GAN loss alongside distribution matching: a discriminator learns to tell real training data from student outputs, and the student gets an extra loss encouraging outputs that look real. This can help the student correct errors in the teacher's distribution rather than only imitating it.

I deliberately left it out to establish a simpler baseline. The primary goal is to match the teacher with fewer steps, not surpass it, so distribution matching alone is a reasonable starting point. A GAN would also mean designing a discriminator for LingBot-VA's dual-stream architecture, deciding whether it scores video, actions, or both, and tuning more hyperparameters, all with limited GPU resources and a small RoboTwin demonstration set where a discriminator could memorize examples. Since my initial students slightly underperform their untrained controls, adding the GAN is a natural follow-up, but those results don't establish that the missing GAN is responsible for the gap.

Training-inference video-conditioning mismatch

During distillation, the action model uses ground-truth future video, but at inference it relies on student-generated video, which may contain errors. This mismatch comes from LingBot-VA's original teacher-forcing approach, which we kept for training stability. It only affects the current video chunk, since previous chunks use real observations.

One possible ablation is to train on detached student-generated video instead, though this could make training less stable.

In short

DMD2 alternates between updating the student using the difference between the teacher's and fake score model's denoising predictions, and updating the fake score model five times to track the student's distribution. This avoids backpropagating through the full sampling process, allowing faster inference with fewer Euler steps. The main question is whether this speedup preserves the teacher's RoboTwin task success rate.

Experiment design

I evaluate four low-NFE variants, two untrained controls and two distilled students at N ∈ {16, 8}, alongside the NFE-30 teacher. Pairing each student with a same-NFE control isolates the effect of distillation from the speedup that comes from simply using fewer steps.

I define NFE N as N denoising steps for both the video and action experts. This keeps the experiment one-dimensional, so all models sit on a single compute-versus-success curve. Since each denoising loop costs roughly N transformer passes, reducing N reduces latency directly. The released checkpoint defaults to 25 video steps and 50 action steps, but I don't explore asymmetric allocations because that would require a much larger sweep. The symmetric convention is applied consistently to the teacher, the controls, and the students so every comparison is under the same compute budget.

Results

Follow-up ablations are still running. Early numbers show small gaps between students and their untrained controls at NFE 16 and NFE 8. Full success-rate and latency tables for all five variants will be added here when the runs finish.

Sources and reproducibility

All code for this project, including the distillation trainer and the eval driver, is at github.com/rding870/robotwin-distill. Everything model- or data-shaped is pinned to an exact revision and recorded in every eval manifest, so any result can be traced back to the exact bits that produced it.

Environment

Python 3.12.3, PyTorch 2.11.0 with CUDA 12.8, transformers 5.5.4, diffusers 0.39.0, and peft 0.21.2 for the LoRA machinery. NumPy is pinned to 1.26.4 for the mplib ABI. The simulator stack is sapien 3.0.0b1 and mplib 0.2.1, both locally patched, plus warp-lang 1.3.1. Everything is resolved with uv 0.9.0 and frozen in a lock file.
